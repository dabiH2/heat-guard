## The three questions, answered up front

| Question | Verdict |
|---|---|
| **Do we have evidence on how supervisors decide today?** | **Yes — far more than expected.** OSHA's rulemaking record contains 12 days of sworn testimony from named contractors describing their exact decision rule, plus two contractor surveys with n=323 and n=298. This is the closest thing to customer discovery that exists without doing customer discovery. |
| **Do we have evidence the app would prevent heat risk?** | **No — and none exists for any tool of this class.** Zero wearable outcome studies worldwide. The only US RCT of a worker heat-decision app was null. The problem is well quantified; the remedy is untested. |
| **Do we have evidence it would prevent unjustified idle time?** | **No — and OSHA's own economics runs the other way.** OSHA models 70–100% of rest-break labour cost as recovered through reduced self-pacing. The over-warning cost has never been measured by anyone, including the industry that complains about it. |

**One consequence that must be acted on:** a sentence in the README is not supportable as
written. See [§5](#5-the-thesis-sentence-that-has-to-change).

## 1. How supervisors actually decide — the numbers

The single best source is the **Dodge/CPWR SmartMarket** contractor survey, fielded
**October–December 2025**, n=323 (153 general contractors, 170 specialty trades).

**Means used by US contractors to assess heat risk** (multi-select):

| Method | Share |
|---|---|
| **Weather forecasts** | **87%** |
| **Heat index (temperature + humidity)** | **84%** |
| Physical demands of the work | 79% |
| Types of clothing / PPE | 62% |
| **Direct jobsite measurement** | **42%** |
| Temperature only | 27% |
| **Mobile heat safety apps (OSHA-NIOSH, AIHA)** | **22%** |
| **Wet bulb globe temperature** | **13%** |
| Wearable sensors | 3% |

Source: [Dodge/CPWR SmartMarket 2026, p.12](https://www.cpwr.com/wp-content/uploads/RR-Dodge_Safety_Management_SmartMarket_2026.pdf) · fielded Oct–Dec 2025

**The trend is away from tooling, not toward it.** CPWR's own framing: *"4 out of 5 options

[SBREFA Panel Report, p.15](https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/Heat-SBREFA-Panel-Report-Full.pdf).

## 2. How supervisors decide — in their own words

All quotes below are from the **OSHA informal public hearing, 16 June – 2 July 2025**, docket
OSHA-2021-0009, unless noted. Master document:
<https://www.regulations.gov/document/OSHA-2021-0009-25560>; transcripts at
`https://downloads.regulations.gov/OSHA-2021-0009-25560/attachment_N.pdf` where N = hearing
day. **This is sworn, attributed, on-the-record testimony from named employers about their own
practice.**

### 2a. The decision rule

> "So what we did is we actually broke it down into four categories: 80 to 90 degree heat
> index, 90 to 105, 105 to 129, and then anything over 129… **anytime there's a heat index that
> hits 129 or above all work is shut down and you need to schedule work for different times of
> the day. So our South Texas crews are pouring concrete mostly at 11 o'clock at night until
> 8 o'clock in the morning** to avoid those high heat index times."
> — **Stephen Kinn**, Senior EHS Manager, **Kwest Group**, testifying with AGC of America
> ([Day 3, pp. 50–51](https://downloads.regulations.gov/OSHA-2021-0009-25560/attachment_3.pdf), 18 Jun 2025)

> "The JHA brings in the project-specific risks on that day, **like what the feels-like
> temperature is and how we'll manage it**… if the feels-like temperature is more than 95
> degrees, then let's have coolers of water out… let's only use a dozer that has AC in it. Or
> if we have to use the one without, let's rotate the operators every 30 minutes… **Maybe,
> let's only get the work done between 7 in the morning and 10 in the morning. That's a plan. A
> prescriptive number on a piece of paper that shuts down a job is not a plan.**"
> — **Richard Steinberg**, **Kinder Morgan**, for the Employers Heat Illness Prevention Coalition
> ([Day 2, pp. 30–31](https://downloads.regulations.gov/OSHA-2021-0009-25560/attachment_2.pdf), 17 Jun 2025)

> "**So they use standard weather apps to be able to monitor where they are during the day. The
> crew lead, essentially, takes responsibility for the safety briefing, the discussion about
> heat… and they're empowered to take as many breaks as they need.**"
> — **Eric Betke**, President, **Farmrail System Inc.**, answering OSHA's direct question about
> how he monitors heat
> ([Day 6, pp. 151–153](https://downloads.regulations.gov/OSHA-2021-0009-25560/attachment_6.pdf), 24 Jun 2025)

## 3. Three findings in the record that are gifts

### 3a. The signal employers act on is *anomaly*, not an absolute threshold

> "The major concentration shouldn't be a prescriptive heat index number, but more of **the
> change** — going from what is a normal heat index in that location to what is a **significant
> change or deviation** from what that normal heat index is in that location. **That's when we
> start seeing our problems on our projects.** And that's where we really concentrate and get
> notice out to our guys… So it's not a uniform, one-size-fits-all. It's look at each location
> and what's significantly different."
> — **Stephen Kinn**, Kwest Group / AGC
> ([Day 3, pp. 15–16](https://downloads.regulations.gov/OSHA-2021-0009-25560/attachment_3.pdf), 18 Jun 2025)

The CISC then formalised exactly this in its post-hearing brief, proposing "**the deviation
from the normal heat index in a particular location**" as the regulatory trigger basis, and
citing **the urban heat island effect** by name as the reason
([CISC brief, pp. 5–6](https://www.agc.org/sites/default/files/Files/Safety%20&%20Health/CORRECTED%20FINAL%20CISC%20Post-Hearing%20Comments%20-%20Docket%20No.%20OSHA-2021-0009%204916-7183-0134%201.pdf)).

**Why this matters to HeatGuard.** The router currently keys off *absolute* OSHA bands. The
buyer, on the record, says the operative signal is *deviation from local normal* — and a
per-site historical API is the only thing that can compute a local normal per site. This is a
product direction validated by the customer before the customer was ever asked.

### 3b. OSHA makes the over-warning argument itself, against its own cost estimate

> "By assuming full, 8-hour work shifts at or above the heat trigger(s), **this methodology may
> overstate the number of breaks employers need to provide since there may be some days where
> the heat triggers are met or exceeded but for shorter periods of time.**"
> — OSHA Preliminary Economic Analysis, footnote 81
> ([Heat-NPRM-Final-Econ-to-Addtl-Requirements.pdf](https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/Heat-NPRM-Final-Econ-to-Addtl-Requirements.pdf))

OSHA states, in writing, that assuming full-shift exceedance overstates the obligation, and
that the fix is knowing **how many hours** the trigger was actually exceeded. It attaches no
dollar value and runs no sensitivity on it. That is the HeatGuard thesis, written by the
regulator, in a footnote, unpriced. **This is the strongest single citation for the
over-warning argument anywhere in the record.**

### 3c. Mobile, multi-site crews are the documented failure mode

> "Many SERs also mentioned that their employees and work crews are **mobile and may work
> multiple work sites in multiple geographical locations.** These SERs shared their concern that
> **tracking the temperature forecasts and relevant heat triggers for the work site that each
> worker or crew is visiting that day is difficult; such tracking becomes a greater challenge if
> each worker or crew visits multiple work sites within a day.**"
> — [SBREFA Panel Report, p.16](https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/Heat-SBREFA-Panel-Report-Full.pdf), Nov 2023

And the burden lands in the wrong place:

> "…the **seemingly constant monitoring would fall directly on our crew foreman, who are
> non-management** and would be on the ground to apply any heat [monitoring]."
> — SER written comment, same report

That is a per-site, per-crew, per-day problem, stated as an unsolved burden by the people who
have it. It is close to a product spec.

### 4a. The problem is well quantified — the forecast really is wrong at the site

**military personnel and marathon runners** (89 FR 70766), transposed to a workplace rule.

### 4c. Unjustified idle time — no evidence, and the counter-argument is stronger

| Finding | Source |
|---|---|
| Cost of precautionary/unnecessary heat work stoppage in construction: **the category is not defined anywhere in the literature or the rulemaking record** | independently confirmed NOT FOUND, twice |
| **OSHA models the opposite.** Rest breaks are *"offsetting about 70 percent of the labor cost of rest breaks"* through recovered pacing; an alternative calculation gives *"up to 100 percent."* **$10.0 bn of a $17.4 bn gross annual cost** is netted out this way | [OSHA PEA](https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/Heat-NPRM-Final-Econ-to-Addtl-Requirements.pdf) |
| Pacing loss employers already absorb without stopping | **14.0 min/shift at HI 80 °F; 40.6 min/shift at HI 90 °F (~8.5% of shift)** for Group 1 establishments | same |
| **The construction industry has never priced its own burden claim.** CISC post-hearing brief (AGC, ABC, NAHB, NRCA + ~25 associations): **zero dollar figures, zero lost-hour figures, zero productivity percentages** | [CISC brief](https://www.agc.org/sites/default/files/Files/Safety%20&%20Health/CORRECTED%20FINAL%20CISC%20Post-Hearing%20Comments%20-%20Docket%20No.%20OSHA-2021-0009%204916-7183-0134%201.pdf) |
| Only US employer survey measuring heat-driven output loss | **~24%** of Texas firms reported reduced revenue/production; n≈350, one month | [Dallas Fed TBOS](https://www.dallasfed.org/research/surveys/tbos/2023/2308q), Aug 2023 |
| Work reduction on hot days | **2.6 fewer minutes per degree above 90 °F** → 26 min lost on a 100 °F day = **5.4% of an 8-hour day** | [Neidell et al., *PLOS ONE*](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0254224), 2003–2018 |

**Read this honestly.** OSHA's position is that heat-related lost time is *already happening*
through self-pacing, and that mandated breaks largely convert unmanaged pacing into managed
rest at little net cost. That undercuts "stop-work is expensive" as a pitch. What it does **not**
undercut is footnote 81 — OSHA agrees that assuming full-shift exceedance overstates the
obligation. **Argue from footnote 81, not from stop-work cost.**

## 5. The thesis sentence that has to change

The README currently says:

> "**Peak temperature is a poor predictor of harm. Duration above a threshold is the signal.**"

**As an empirical claim about predicting worker harm, this is not supportable.** What the
literature actually shows:

| Evidence | Result |
|---|---|
| Studies using "hours above threshold X" as an exposure variable for occupational heat illness/injury | **None exist.** Europe PMC exact-phrase: 0 hits |
| The one worker study testing within-day hourly WBGT | **NULL on all three metrics** — hour-of-injury OR 1.00 (p=0.87); lagged OR 1.01 (p=0.71); change OR 0.96 (p=0.35). [PubMed 36650099](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36650099/) |
| The one study that asked peak-vs-cumulative directly (military) | **Same-day WBGT term ~3× the prior-day term** — OR 1.10/°F vs 1.03/°F. Cumulation *adds*; it does not displace. [PubMed 15632673](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15632673/) |
| Largest US test of intensity vs duration | **Intensity significant (2.49%/°F, p<0.01); duration NOT significant (0.38%/day)**. [PMC3040608](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3040608/) — **[GEN POP], not workers** |
| Best US worker-injury study to date, on its own limitation | *"We also used daily mean heat index and **did not account for hour-specific exposure**"* — [DOI 10.1186/s12940-025-01231-1](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12940-025-01231-1) |

**The defensible replacement claim.** Duration is not a better *predictor* — that is untested.
Duration is **the structure every occupational heat limit already uses**:

- **NIOSH RAL and REL** are hour-resolved work/rest curves: *"RALs and RELs take work/rest
  schedules into consideration so are shown for 60, 45, 30, and 15 minute time periods of
  work"*, and both are *"expressed as 1-hour time-weighted averages"* —
  [NIOSH 2016-106, §8.1](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/docs/2016-106/pdfs/2016-106.pdf)
- **NIOSH deleted its ceiling (peak) limit in 2016** — *"the decision to remove the ceiling
  limit recommendations"* — same source
- **ISO 7933:2023**'s actual output is an allowable exposure *time*: *"determines the maximum
  allowable exposure times within which the physiological strain is acceptable for 95% of the
  exposed population"* — [ISO 7933:2023](https://www.iso.org/standard/78240.html)
- **ACGIH TLV** is structured as WBGT × 100/75/50/25% work per hour
- **OSHA's proposed triggers** are defined against the NIOSH RAL and REL — i.e. against
  duration-structured limits (89 FR 70698)

**Suggested rewrite:**

> Every occupational heat limit in force — NIOSH's RAL and REL, ACGIH's TLV, ISO 7933 — is
> defined as time at a condition, and NIOSH removed its peak-temperature ceiling limit
> in 2016. A daily peak cannot be compared against any of them. Hours above a threshold can.

That claim is airtight, it is about standards architecture rather than contested epidemiology,
and it survives a judge who knows the literature. **Say plainly that duration has not been shown
to out-predict peak — nobody has tested it — and that this is a gap, not a finding.**

## 6. Counter-evidence you must be ready for

A judge or a buyer will find these. Better to have the answer prepared.

| Objection, with its source | The answer |
|---|---|
| **A contractor testified the free NWS forecast is sufficient.** Sonya Owens, Cleanwerks: *"The National Weather Service produces a weather app… if you click on any day, you would see hourly temperatures… **This is all I need to change work hours, days off, job assignments, production, and timelines.**"* ([Day 12, pp. 129–130](https://downloads.regulations.gov/OSHA-2021-0009-25560/attachment_12.pdf)) | She is describing planning, not per-site exposure accounting. The same record contains the measured answer: the OSHA-NIOSH app, fed by the same NWS data, detected **0%** of high and extreme risk conditions against on-site WBGT. Sufficiency of feel is not sufficiency of measurement. |
| **The industry asked OSHA for *less* measurement, not more.** CISC: WBGT equipment is *"not only expensive, but also complex… requires regular calibration"*; asked for *"more practical methods… such as ambient temperature and heat index."* | This is the strongest argument for HeatGuard's form factor. It is an **API, not a sensor** — no hardware, no calibration, no per-site instrument. It gives site-resolved data with zero of the burden the industry objects to. Lead with this. |
| **Only 42% do any direct jobsite measurement, and app use fell 35%→22%.** | Correct, and it is the market risk. Contractors are not currently buying measurement. The wedge is not measurement-for-its-own-sake but the **record** — Appendix I asks how you monitored, and 1,392 hazard alert letters vs 60 citations shows documented process is what separates outcomes. |
| **OSHA thinks lost time is mostly recovered anyway** (70–100% pacing offset). | Concede it, and argue from footnote 81 instead — OSHA's own admission that assuming full-shift exceedance overstates the obligation. |
| **Written heat policies are often not operative.** NIOSH found a policy with numeric work/rest triggers that no supervisor enforced and no worker followed. | A decision-support tool that nobody opens has the same failure mode. This is a real threat to adoption and should be named as a limitation, not argued away. |

## 7. What could not be sourced

1. **A peer-reviewed US survey of construction *foremen/supervisors*** reporting how they make
   the go/no-go call. PubMed, PMC, ScienceDirect, MDPI, ASCE searched. Dodge/CPWR respondents
   are C-level (31%), VP/director (28%), manager (29%) — **not field foremen**.
2. **Any US study of employer behavioural response to NWS heat advisories** — no timecard,
   payroll, or jobsite-activity data on alert vs non-alert days exists.
3. **Direct measurement of what share of US contractors shift start times.** Widely asserted,
   only proxied by a survey item that bundles scheduling with other administrative controls.
4. **A national US employer survey on heat-driven days lost.** AGC's and ABC's own annual
   surveys do not ask — the word "weather" does not appear in AGC's 2023/2024/2025 workforce
   surveys.
5. **A named contractor giving a concrete number of days or hours lost to heat.**
6. **NIOSH Health Hazard Evaluations at agricultural, landscaping, utility or waste worksites**
   — none exist. Outdoor heat HHEs cover parks, roofing, street services, ports of entry and
   wildland fire only.
7. **City of Phoenix's own heat prevention plans for its outdoor departments** — not published;
   the 2025 plan lists "Resources / More Info: N/A". Would require a records request.
8. **Any study comparing heat illness rates between forecast-guided and on-site-measured
   worksites.** The link this product depends on has never been tested by anyone.
9. **CPWR's contractor heat survey is currently in the field** — no results published as of
   29 Aug 2026. Flag as forthcoming.

**Two figures to avoid.** The "43.7% acclimatize / 34% change working hours" numbers circulate
as construction figures but come from a scoping review of **migrant farmworkers**. And a
machine-generated paper titled *"Hot Standards, Cool Workers?"* ranks highly in search and
claims a "12% reduction" — it has no author, no data, and its host site states that *"every
single paper contains errors or defects."* Do not cite it.

## 8. What can honestly be claimed

> The problem is documented and quantified; the remedy is untested — and that is the same
> position OSHA is in.

Three claims survive scrutiny:

1. **Supervisors decide from the public forecast, not from site measurement.** 87% use weather
   forecasts, 42% do any direct jobsite measurement, 13% use WBGT (n=323, Oct–Dec 2025), and
   the industry told OSHA that a monitoring mandate would make them *"full-time weathermen."*
2. **The forecast is measurably wrong at the worksite, worst where it matters.** The OSHA-NIOSH
   app detected **0%** of high and extreme risk conditions against on-site WBGT; NWS WBGT
   forecasts are cold-biased **−1.6 °C** at the highest WBGTs; worksite heat index ran **~7.3 °C**
   above the regional station for Florida farmworkers.
3. **Duration is the structure every occupational heat limit already uses**, and OSHA itself
   concedes in footnote 81 that assuming full-shift exceedance overstates what employers owe.

And one thing must be said out loud, because a judge will otherwise say it first:

> **No tool of this kind has ever been shown to reduce heat illness.** Zero wearable outcome
> studies exist worldwide; the only US RCT of a worker heat-decision app was null on
> physiological strain; the one strong positive result (−21.9% construction injuries, Italy
> 2024) came from a legally enforced work ban, not from the forecasting platform that triggered
> it. HeatGuard closes a measurement gap that is well documented. Whether closing it changes
> outcomes is unmeasured — for HeatGuard and for every competing approach.

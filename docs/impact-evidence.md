# Impact & Relevance — sourced evidence pack

*Compiled 29 August 2026. Every figure below carries a source URL and the year the **data**
refers to. Nothing here is estimated, interpolated, or recalled. Where a figure does not
exist, it is marked **NOT FOUND** rather than approximated.*

## How to read this

Three conventions, applied without exception:

| Tag | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **[OCC]** | Figure is about **outdoor workers / occupational exposure**. |
| **[GEN POP]** | Figure is about the **general population**. Not a worker figure. Do not present it as one. |
| **[STALE: YYYY]** | Most recent available data is older than three years. |

**Conflating [OCC] and [GEN POP] is the single most common error in this space, and the one a
judge is most likely to catch.** Maricopa County's death counts are the trap: they are large,
local, and vivid, and they are **not** worker deaths. The county does not collect occupation.

Two figures were corrected during verification and the wrong versions are listed in
[§7 Traps](#7-traps-figures-that-are-widely-miscited) so they are not reintroduced later.

### 2a. What the rules actually say about *measurement* — the opening

This is the part of the regulatory record that matters most for HeatGuard, because it is where
the regulator names the gap in its own words.

**OSHA's proposed rule permits forecast-only monitoring outdoors** (89 FR 70698, §(d)(1)):

> "(i) **Tracking local heat index forecasts provided by the National Weather Service** or
> other reputable sources; **or** (ii) At or as close as possible to the work area(s),
> measuring: (A) Heat index… or (B) Wet bulb globe temperature."

**OSHA's own Technical Manual says why that is not equivalent:**

> "WBGT measurement will capture radiant heat and wind reduction impacts from worksite
> structures or heat sources that **historic weather from a remote location may not
> represent**."
> — [OSHA Technical Manual, Section III Chapter 4](https://www.osha.gov/otm/section-3-health-hazards/chapter-4), updated 15 Sep 2017

> "WBGT measurements are **most reliable when taken at, or as close as possible to, the work
> area**. When a worker moves between two or more areas with different environmental
> conditions, or when the **conditions vary substantially in the work area**, assess the heat
> hazard using representative measurements for the different conditions." — same source

**NIOSH says measurements should be hourly and at the worker's position:**

> "Environmental heat measurements should be made **at or as close as feasible to the work
> area where the worker is exposed** and represents the environmental heat conditions **at the
> worker's position**… should be made **at least hourly**, during the hottest portion of each
> work shift."
> — [NIOSH 2016-106](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/docs/2016-106/), Feb 2016 · [STALE: 2016] *(a
> recommendation, not a dataset — staleness is less material here)*

**The current NEP warns that the forecast threshold is not protective:**

> "Heat-related fatalities have occurred with a **heat index below 80 °F**, particularly when
> aggravating factors are present."
> — [CPL 03-00-024, §X](https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/enforcement/directives/CPL_03-00-024_0.pdf), 10 Apr 2026

**And what OSHA asks an employer during an inspection is open-ended:**

> Appendix I, Q2: "**How did the employer monitor ambient temperature(s) and levels of work
> exertion at the worksite?**" — same directive

No method is prescribed. Arizona's own employer template
([Example Arizona Heat Illness Prevention Plan](https://www.azica.gov/sites/default/files/2026-04/Example%20Arizona%20Heat%20Illness%20Prevention%20Plan.pdf),
2026) goes further: its only numeric trigger is §5.1 "High Heat Procedures (**Heat Index ≥
100 °F**)", its monitoring instruction is "Supervisors monitor weather conditions daily", and
it contains **no thermometer, no WBGT, and no on-site measurement instruction of any kind**.

### On row 3.4 — the only defensible way to price the cost of over-warning

HeatGuard's `$55/h loaded rate` slider is currently an assumption with no citation. **$51.23/h
(BLS ECEC, construction industry, March 2026) is a real, current, citable number and should
replace it as the slider's default**, with the source shown in the UI. That converts the
"$35,000 of phantom stop-work" claim from an invented rate into an arithmetic step a judge can
check:

> 643 phantom worker-hours × $51.23/h = **$32,941**

**The lower bound, and where its 12.5% comes from.** Row 2.21a: at the proposed high heat
trigger an employer owes a minimum 15-minute paid rest break at least every two hours. 15 min
per 120 min = **12.5% of paid time**. So the labour cost of mis-scoping which hours sit above
the trigger, valued only as mandated rest that was not actually owed, is:

> 643 worker-hours × 12.5% = 80.4 worker-hours × $51.23/h = **$4,118**

That is the floor — what you owe *even while continuing to work*. It is the bound that survives
the objection that a heat trigger does not permit you to stop work anyway (§2a). The $32,941
upper bound assumes full idle labour, which is the aggressive reading. **Quote the range, not
either end alone**, and note that the proposed rule is not in force (row 2.18), so this is what
the obligation would cost under the rule as proposed.

Use `$51.23` and `≈$33,000`, not `$55` and `≈$35,000`. The smaller, sourced number is worth
more than the larger, invented one. Be explicit that this values *idle loaded labour*, which
is a defensible proxy for the cost of an unnecessary stop-work and **not** a measured cost of
stoppage — no such measured figure exists (see §6.8).

### Step 4 — What this count is and is not

- **It counts jobs, not people who are outdoors at any given moment.** BLS OEWS counts
  employment by occupation; it does not say what fraction of a shift is spent outdoors.
- **It is a lower bound on outdoor exposure.** It omits agriculture (except where noted),
  utilities field staff outside 49-905x, delivery drivers, roofers working under other SOC
  codes, public-works and parks crews, and every self-employed worker — **OEWS excludes the
  self-employed entirely**, which is material in construction and landscaping.
- **It is not a customer count.** These workers are not buyers. The buyer is their employer's
  safety function; how many employers that is, and what they would pay, is **NOT FOUND** and
  should not be guessed at. That is what customer discovery is for.
- **API coverage is not assumed.** The FortyGuard API returns zero tiles for areas outside the
  US and, as measured in `docs/api-notes.md`, for some US sites on some dates — one of the
  twelve Phoenix sites returned zero tiles on the demo date. The sixteen metros above are a
  list of hot US metros, **not** a verified coverage map. Claiming coverage of all 1,364,770
  would overstate what has been tested.

Each of these was searched for and does not exist in citable form. They are listed so nobody
fills them in later with a plausible-sounding number.

1. **Arizona occupational heat deaths, any year, any source.** BLS's Arizona CFOI release
   publishes no environmental-heat line. ADOSH's own SEP (Apr 2026) cites only *national*
   BLS 2015–2019 figures and gives no Arizona number.
2. **Maricopa County work-related heat deaths.** Not collected — no occupation, industry or
   work-relatedness variable exists in the report (row 1.17).
3. **A heat-specific workers'-comp claim cost published by NCCI itself.** NCCI's heat research
   is frequency-only; its public Injury Characteristics dashboard has no heat category. The
   only NCCI-derived heat dollar figure in the public domain is the one routed through OSHA
   $afety Pays (row 3.1), and it is 2015–2017 data.
4. **A heat-illness claim cost from California DIR/DWC, Texas DWC, or Washington L&I
   directly.** CDPH's occupational HRI brief (2000–2022) reports 20,564 claims and rates by
   occupation with **zero dollar figures**.
5. **Any heat-illness workers'-comp claim cost with a data year of 2023 or later.** Every
   dollar figure located is 2017 or older.
6. **A US-specific per-degree construction labour productivity loss from peer-reviewed work.**
   The 2024 BMC Public Health meta-analysis (14 studies, 2,387 construction workers) is
   global; its per-degree figures are Beijing and Indian brick-kiln workers.
7. **A present-day US construction heat productivity figure in dollars or lost hours.** UCS's
   $14.4 bn is a *midcentury projection*, not a current figure. The SF Fed gives only
   2100/2200 percentages. Atlantic Council treats construction qualitatively only.
8. **Any figure for the cost of precautionary work stoppage — the cost of over-warning.**
   Searched government, insurer and peer-reviewed sources. Everything returned was vendor
   marketing or unsourced trade commentary. **This does not exist**, which is itself worth
   saying: the expensive error in this domain is the one nobody has measured.
9. **A named, dated, court-documented heat wrongful-death settlement with a dollar figure.**
   The $4.5 M figure circulating online comes from a law-firm case-results page with no case
   name, docket, or date. Use the OSHA enforcement figure (row 2.14) instead.

12. **Cumulative OSHA heat inspection and GDC citation counts after 29 Dec 2024.** The April

13. **A direct BLS headcount of US workers who work primarily outdoors.** BLS publishes a

## 7. Traps — figures that are widely miscited

Verified against primary sources; each of these is wrong and each appears in circulation.

| Miscitation | Reality |
|---|---|
| "Maricopa County had 425 heat deaths in 2025" | **430.** 425 is the preliminary count with 9 cases still under investigation, carried in the City of Phoenix draft plan. MCDPH's final April 2026 report says 430. |
| "2023 was the record year with 625" | **645.** The 625 appears in the Phoenix draft plan and matches no MCDPH publication. |
| "3 out of 5 Maricopa heat deaths were outdoors" | **74%** (313 of 430). The "3 out of 5" appears in the report's own p5 infographic and contradicts its Table B. Cite the table. |
| "A heat injury costs $35,000" | That is the **all-injury** average social cost of a workplace injury (Leigh 2011, 2021 dollars), used in Park/Pankratz/Behrer. It is **not** heat-specific. |
| "Heat costs the US $100 bn a year, per the Atlantic Council" | The ~$98 bn is Parsons et al. 2022, a **global** study, 2017 data, US line item, **all workers** — not the Atlantic Council and not construction. |
| "NWS HeatRisk is a 2.5 km product" | No NWS HeatRisk document states a resolution; 2.5 km is the **NDFD** grid. The "2.5" in `HeatRisk-v2.5-Overview.pdf` is a **version number**. Cite NDFD for the resolution. |
| "The OSHA heat NEP expires April 2025 / 2026" | The 2022 NEP was extended once to 8 Apr 2026 and then **superseded on 10 Apr 2026** by a revised directive running five years. Citing the 2022 or 2025 documents is citing an OSHA archive page. |
| "A federal heat standard is imminent" | As of 29 Aug 2026 the record has been closed since 30 Oct 2025. The Unified Agenda's next milestone is a **Supplemental** NPRM in Dec 2026; final action is projected Oct 2027 with **no legal deadline**. |
| "Arizona's heat SEP sets a 100 °F trigger" | The 100 °F trigger is in the standalone *Example Arizona Heat Illness Prevention Plan*, not in the SEP directive. Attributing it to the SEP will not check out. |
